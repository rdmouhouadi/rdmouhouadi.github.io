What is a home’s energy rating worth?

Personal project · Open data · 2026 · Part 1 of 2: the model

New to me: DuckDB, Polars, uv, ADEME’s remote database, and joining two datasets with no shared key · First result: same day · Whole model: one day; the MLOps half is part 2

I built a model that estimates the sale price of a French home from public data, and used it to measure what the energy rating (DPE) adds. On 2025 sales it never saw, half of its estimates fall within 16% of the real price. On identical sales, adding the energy rating cuts the median error by 13%.

Context

France publishes every property sale: price, date, surface, address [1]. It also publishes every energy performance diagnosis (DPE) made since July 2021, the A-to-G rating a seller must show [2]. Buyers and sellers keep asking the same question: how much is a better rating worth?

The two datasets were never designed to work together. Sales records have no energy rating, and ratings have no sale price. There is no shared identifier between them.

The rating itself keeps changing. On 1 January 2026, the conversion coefficient for electricity in the DPE calculation dropped from 2.3 to 1.9 [4], and it drops again to 1.7 on 1 January 2027 [5]. The same home can get a different label without any work being done.

Problem

My first idea was forecasting household energy use from smart meters. I dropped it: the people who would use such a tool don’t have the meter data to feed it. A price estimate works because anyone knows their commune, surface and number of rooms.

Estimators already exist, and so does a dataset that links sales to energy ratings [3]. What I found no one covering is the rating rules themselves changing, as they did in January 2026 and will again in January 2027. A model trained on old labels then quietly misreads new ones. That became the long-term goal: a model that notices the change and retrains itself.

I wanted a tool people could actually use: enter what you know about a home and get an estimated price with an honest error. That rules out inputs nobody has, and it means testing on future sales, not on a random sample of the past.

Research question: after joining the two public datasets, how accurately can a model estimate a sale price, and how much does the energy rating improve the estimate?

Methodology

Data

Sales come from the geolocated DVF files for 2021 to 2025 [1]. One sale is spread over several rows, one per lot or plot, so I rebuilt one row per sale and kept ordinary sales of exactly one house or apartment. Outliers in price per m² were trimmed within each département and property type. That left 4,245,617 sales.

Energy ratings come from ADEME’s database [2], a relational model of more than 150 tables published as a remote DuckDB file. I extracted the columns I needed into Parquet and flattened them to one row per rating: 15,672,018 ratings for existing houses and apartments.

Joining sales and ratings

With no shared key, the match has to be rebuilt from what both sides record. I normalised addresses on both sides (no accents or punctuation, abbreviations expanded) and kept a pair only if all of these held:

  • same commune, house number, street and property type;
  • living area within ±10%;
  • rating dated between 12 months before and 1 month after the sale;
  • exactly one candidate on each side, so that one rating matches one sale.

The result is 604,773 matched sales. An independent dataset built with nearly the same rules reports 610,000 [3]. The two counts agree within 1%, which is the best check available that the join is sound. Matches are rare in 2021, because the new rating only started in July 2021, then stable at 14–22% of sales per year.

Features

Location explains most of a price, so the key features describe the local market: the average log price per m² over the previous 12 months, for the same property type, in three nested areas. Those areas are the cadastral section (roughly a neighbourhood, read from the first 10 characters of the parcel ID), the commune and the département. Each average uses only sales before the one being estimated, so no information from the answer leaks into the inputs. A section with few past sales is pulled toward its commune average, and a commune toward its département, so that small samples do not dominate.

The other features are what an owner knows: property type, living area, rooms, land, outbuildings, location. The rating features are the class, CO₂ class, energy use per m², construction period and year, heating type and floor. They are empty when a sale has no matched rating. Because ADEME also publishes each rating recalculated under the 1.9 coefficient [2], every class in the training data follows the same rule.

Protocol

The split is by time, never random: training on sales before July 2024, validation on the second half of 2024, test on all of 2025. Models were compared and features selected on validation only. The chosen setup was then refit on training and validation together, and scored on the test set once.

Implementation

Python on one laptop, with memory capped at 5 GB: DuckDB for the large joins, Polars for cleaning, LightGBM, XGBoost and scikit-learn for the models, uv for the environment. The two raw sources total about 20 million rows. Spark would have added setup without benefit at this size.

The memory cap shaped the code. My first attempt pulled the energy ratings in a single query and ran out of memory. The rewrite works in two stages: first copy only the needed columns of each table to local Parquet files, then join them locally in eight hash buckets. It extracts all 15.7 million ratings in under three minutes within 5 GB. The sales files needed care too: automatic type detection broke on house numbers such as “14A”, so every column is read as text and converted explicitly.

The code is organised as a Python package (data, features, models, monitoring), with structured logs that tag every run and its metrics. The repository documents the architecture, six decision records, a data dictionary, the data lineage and a model card.

Results

Choosing a model

Five approaches were compared on all sales, without rating features. The baseline does no learning: it predicts the neighbourhood’s past average.

Gradient-boosted trees win, ahead of a random forest [8], as is usual on tabular data [9]. LightGBM [6] and XGBoost [7] are tied on accuracy, and LightGBM trains in a third of the time, which matters once retraining is automated. A linear model barely beats the baseline: it cannot capture that surface or land are worth different amounts in different places.

What the energy rating adds

To isolate the rating’s effect, I trained the same model twice on the same matched sales from 2022 onward, once without the rating features and once with them.

Table 2. Same model, same rows (73,344 validation sales with a matched rating).
FeaturesMedian errorWithin 20%Mean error
Without rating14.3%65.2%€45,075
With rating12.5%71.3%€40,224

The rating cuts the median error by 13% (14.3% to 12.5%). Matched sales are easier to estimate than average, even without the rating, because the match itself confirms their address and surface. So these numbers should only be compared with each other, never with Table 1.

Which features matter

The neighbourhood’s past prices dominate. Among the rating features, the construction period matters more than the energy class itself: a building’s age carries its structure, style and typical condition. Three features scored zero or within noise and were dropped, including the time index. Trees cannot extrapolate: every validation date lies after every training date, so the model treats them all as the latest month. The market trend is already carried by the rolling 12-month prices.

Final test on 2025

The final model is trained on all sales, with the rating features empty when there is no match, so one model serves owners who know their rating and those who don’t. Early stopping picked 2,646 trees, and the model was refit on 3.45 million sales (2021–2024).

The test score (16.0%) is as good as validation (16.3%), so the model holds up on a year it never saw. Apartments are easier than houses, which vary in ways the data does not record: land quality, condition, extensions.

Takeaway

The join was the project. The model was quick to train. The time went into rebuilding a link the two datasets never had, and into checking it against an independent count. Once it held, the energy rating’s value could be measured on identical sales, instead of being guessed from averages.

Limitations

  • Sales records hold no condition, floor for houses, view or renovation, so part of every price is out of reach.
  • Alsace, Moselle and Mayotte are not in the sales data.
  • Only 14% of sales have a matched rating. Matched sales are cleaner than average, so the “with rating” score is partly selection.
  • The rating’s effect is an association learned from local sales, not a guaranteed gain from renovating.
  • Ratings issued from 2027 use a new coefficient [5]. Until the model is retrained, new labels will not mean what the training labels meant.
